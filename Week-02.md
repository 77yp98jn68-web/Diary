##  Audio Guides

This week I looked into the topic of audio guides and started by searching for what is available for Ulm Münster. In doing so, I came across an article from 2017 about an app that guides you around Ulm Münster. Unfortunately, I couldn't find the app itself, but perhaps we could ask the staff at Ulm Münster whether they have more detailed information. Another website that deals with Ulm Münster is the Ulm Schuldek website, which offers several short audio clips on how Ulm Münster came to be built. It also has floor plans and short worksheets on the construction history. However, the site is not visually appealing and its navigation is not very intuitive. I also found several audio guides covering the city of Ulm as a whole. The app Audiala offers an audio tour of all of Ulm. What stood out about this app is that it is highly personalized, for example in terms of pace and narrator voice. That is something we had also considered offering in our project, having different voices, perhaps including one for children narrated by the Ulmer Spatz. Unfortunately, the app is not free. Other digital city guides, some of which also offer audio, were Wanderlog, which was also free, as well as VoiceMap and WeGoTrip, which are not free and cost around 10 euros. One app that has nothing to do with Ulm but is very helpful for the project is Hidden Florence, which lets you explore Florence. The app starts with a short audio introduction that leads straight into the topic. Then, when you are in Florence, you can choose from several tours, each showing how long it takes, how many stops it has, and what its theme is. Once you have chosen a tour, you can download it and use it offline. A city map then opens with the individual stops marked on it, and you can listen to the introduction. As you walk to the stops, you can tap on each one and an audio clip about that stop plays. Another good feature is that some routes are available in several languages. Overall, this app comes closest to the idea we had for Ulm Münster.


## Master Builders 

Over its 500-year history, 22 master builders (Münsterbaumeister) directed the construction and upkeep of Ulm Minster.
The story boils down to four key people who actually shaped what you see today:

- Heinrich II Parler (1377 – late 1380s): Laid the foundation stone in 1377 and set up the original layout as a standard Gothic hall church.
- Ulrich von Ensingen (1392–1419): Had the wild vision to make it huge. He redesigned the church into a soaring basilica and planned the      massive central tower to outshine every other church in Europe.
- Burkhart Engelberg (1494–1512): Saved the whole thing from falling down. When pushing the tower higher caused massive cracks in the         walls, he stepped in, strengthened the foundation, and added extra interior columns to carry the weight.
- August Beyer (1885–1890): Finished the job. After work stopped for centuries, he led the final 19th-century push and placed the top stone   cross at 161.53 meters (530 ft), giving Ulm the tallest church spire in the world.

## Ulma-Ulm-Ulmensis

In the book Ulma-Ulm-Ulmensis from Albert Schmid I found a lot of interesting facts about the Ulm Minster and about the building process: 

The construction of Ulm Minster began because the citizens wanted to build a large church where all the citizens could fit inside. The plans had existed for some time, but an unsuccessful siege by Emperor Charles IV in 1376 helped motivate the project.
The foundation stone was laid on 30 June 1377 at 7 a.m., shortly after sunrise. Almost the entire population of Ulm witnessed the ceremony. Mayor Kraft donated 100 gold guilders on that day.

Originally, a five-metre-high statue of the Virgin Mary holding the Christ Child was planned for the top of the tower.

Many builders worked on the church over the years. The church was opened in 1405, but it was not finished yet.
Matthäus Böblinger was responsible for the tower's construction from 1474 to 1494. The tower had reached a height of 70.46 metres. After structural problems, Burkhart Engelberg took over the repairs and continued the construction work in 1453. He later strengthened the tower's foundations.

A large stone bird, originally intended to represent the imperial eagle, eventually became associated with the famous Ulmer Spatz, which is now a symbol of the city.

In 1844, people started building again to finish the church. They collected money from different parts of Germany.
The southern tower was completed in 1877, and the northern tower in 1880.
The main tower was finally completed on 31 May 1890 at 6 p.m., when the final stone was put into place.

At that time, Ulm Minster became famous for having the tallest church tower in the world.
- Main tower: 161.53 metres high
- East towers: 86 metres high
- Length: 139.5 metres
- Width: 59.2 metres
